NASA Space Apps 2026: Lunar Landing Site Navigator

Will this landing site and date still work if the launch slips?

Lunario is our entry for the NASA Space Apps challenge Lunar Landing Site Navigator. It uses JPL Horizons and LOLA terrain data to compare sunlight and Earth visibility at the Moon’s south pole, so you can see straight away whether a site and date still work, and what to change if they don’t. Open the Dashboard to try it.

Why it matters

Two weeks late can turn a good site into a dark one.

At Connecting Ridge, a 14-day stay from 29 June 2027 has sunlight 97.6% of the time, and the longest dark gap is 8 hours. On a smooth Moon with no terrain, the same stay would get none. Arrive on 13 July instead and sunlight drops to 16.1%, with a dark gap of 9 days, and the energy check fails. Keep the new date, change only the site to de Gerlache–Shackleton, and sunlight is back to 95.5%.

That’s the comparison Lunario is built for: move the date or the site, and see whether the mission still has power and a view of Earth.

What Lunario does

Pick a site and date
Choose one of the prepared south pole sites, an arrival date and a length of stay.
Read the real horizon
Sun and Earth positions come from JPL Horizons and are set against a horizon traced from LOLA terrain, so crater rims and ridges block the view where they actually rise.
See the windows
How much of the stay has sunlight, how much has Earth in view, and the longest gap in each, on a timeline, a horizon chart and a south pole map. Every result sits next to the smooth-Moon figure, so you can see what the terrain changes.
Compare a slip
Move the arrival date or switch to a nearby site, and compare the options side by side against a simple energy check.
Three modes
Public for a quick comparison, Educator for classroom use, and Planner for the full detail.

The NASA data behind it

JPL Horizons
Sun and Earth positions for each hour of the stay. Lunario’s calculations are checked against Horizons to within 0.005°.
LOLA terrain
Elevation data from the Lunar Orbiter Laser Altimeter, used to trace the local horizon at each prepared site.
Why not the illumination maps
NASA’s published illumination maps are long-term averages. Lunario recalculates sunlight from the terrain and the ephemeris for the specific stay instead, so a slipped date can be judged on its own.

Limits

Educational, not certified
Lunario is a screening and teaching tool, not an operational planning system. The best result it gives is “conditional”, never a full pass.
Nine prepared sites
Precise LOLA horizons are prepared for 9 sites. A custom point borrows the horizon of the nearest one, and the app shows how far away that is.
Terrain detail
The terrain grid is 40 metres, which smooths out the sharpest ridges.
Hourly steps
Sunrise and sunset can be out by up to an hour, so they are shown as ranges.
A generic lander
The energy check uses a generic lander (a 4 m² solar array, a 250 W load and a 10 kWh battery), not a named vehicle.

The challenge

Missions to the Moon’s south pole face sharp swings in sunlight and Earth visibility. The Sun decides power, and Earth’s place in the sky decides whether a lander can talk home without a relay. NASA’s Commercial Lunar Payload Services (CLPS) program and other missions need to compare sites and dates quickly, especially when a launch slips.

The data already exists in tools such as NASA’s Quickmap, Moon Trek and JPL Horizons, but they are built for detailed analysis rather than quick side-by-side comparison. The Lunar Landing Site Navigator brief asked for an intuitive tool to do that.
